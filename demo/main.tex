\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\sectionopening{A scholarly page is an argument made visible,} and its hierarchy
tells the reader where to begin, how long to stay, and when a claim has earned
support.
Good typography does not compete with that argument.
It gives the argument a stable surface on which to appear.

\textcite{butterick2019practical} describes this reader-first discipline as a
practical part of writing rather than a decorative afterthought.
The same idea appears in the more exacting account of spacing and proportion by
\textcite{hochuli1987detail}.
Lanepaper v3 brings those concerns together in a deliberately small package:
the document keeps ordinary LaTeX structures, while the package gives those
structures a consistent typographic voice.

\safeparagraph{A small claim}
This paper argues that a useful typography package should be most visible in
the way a page holds together, not in the number of commands a document must
learn.
The examples that follow therefore form one short discussion rather than a
catalogue.
They show a hierarchy, a list, a quotation, a figure, a table, a footnote, a
mathematical display, and citations as parts of the same argument.

The page specimen in \cref{fig:page-rhythm} and the compact comparison in
\cref{tab:signals} make the central choices visible.
The conclusion returns to the boundary between package-owned typography and
document-owned content.

\section{Hierarchy Before Decoration}
\label{sec:hierarchy}

\sectionopening{Hierarchy is a promise about attention,} and a good promise is
quiet enough to keep.
The largest heading should establish a new subject, a subsection should narrow
it, and a paragraph should carry the thought without forcing the reader to
relearn the page.

\subsection{Headings as promises}
\label{subsec:headings}

\paragraph{A readable order.}
The article class already supplies the familiar sectioning structure.
Lanepaper refines its scale, weight, colour, and spacing without asking the
author to replace \verb|\section| or \verb|\subsection| with a private
vocabulary.
That distinction matters because the structure remains legible to editors,
converters, screen readers, and future authors.

\subsubsection{Scale without spectacle}
The most effective hierarchy is relational.
Readers understand a heading because it differs from the text around it, not
because it becomes an ornament.
Three cues do most of the work on a normal page:

%% FIX (#102): Exercise the package's retained nested list marker hierarchy.
\begin{itemize}
  \item position separates a new subject from the paragraph that precedes it;
  \item size and weight signal the level of the subject;
    \begin{itemize}
      \item scale separates a section from its paragraph;
        \begin{itemize}
          \item a quiet difference is enough when the hierarchy is sound.
        \end{itemize}
    \end{itemize}
  \item restrained colour keeps the levels distinct without making colour carry
    the meaning.
\end{itemize}

The result is a page that can be scanned at a distance and read closely without
changing character.

that a reader encounters in a real article.

\section{Rhythm in Practice}
\label{sec:rhythm}

\sectionopening{Hierarchy opens the door,} but rhythm determines whether the
reader can remain inside.
Paragraph indentation, display spacing, captions, and footnotes are small
decisions when considered separately.
Together they decide whether a page feels composed or merely assembled.

\subsection{A quotation can make room}
\label{subsec:quotation}

Longer words need room around them, and longer thoughts sometimes need a change
of measure.
The standard \texttt{quote} environment supplies that change without inventing
a special quotation wrapper.

\begin{quote}
  A page should make room for the argument before it makes room for itself.
  \hfill--- An editor's maxim
\end{quote}

The quotation is set apart, but it remains part of the article's flow.
That is the useful kind of emphasis: a change in measure that clarifies the
relationship between voices without interrupting the reader's sense of place.

\subsection{A table should clarify}
\label{subsec:table}

Tables are most helpful when they reduce a repeated explanation to a form that
can be compared at a glance.
\Cref{tab:signals} is intentionally small.
Its entries are an editorial description of the page, not measurements from a
separate experiment.

The table follows two ordinary conventions.
Its caption appears above the content, and the booktabs rules are horizontal

Those conventions are modest, but they remove visual noise precisely where
readers are trying to compare things.

\subsection{A footnote can stay in the margin of attention}
\label{subsec:footnote}

Footnotes are useful when they preserve the sentence's momentum while offering
context to readers who want it.
This note demonstrates that secondary voice without turning the page into a
second column.\footnote{The note is deliberately brief: a demonstration should
show the relationship between the body and the footnote, not bury the argument
under commentary.}
The main text can then continue without a parenthetical detour.

\subsection{Mathematics needs the same measure}
\label{subsec:mathematics}

Mathematics is another change of visual register, but it should still belong to
the prose around it.
Suppose a reader's attention is represented by a simple ratio of hierarchy
\(H\), rhythm \(R\), and distraction \(D\).
The notation is only illustrative, but it makes the relationship explicit:

\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{Q} = \frac{H + R}{D},
  \qquad H, R, D \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}.
  \label{eq:attention}
\end{equation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:attention} is not a metric for publishing quality.
It is a reminder that typography works as a system.
If hierarchy and rhythm support one another, the reader spends less attention
decoding the page and more attention following the claim.

\section{Structure in Use}
\label{sec:structure}

\sectionopening{A coherent article moves between forms,} and its forms should
serve the argument rather than announce their own implementation.
The document therefore owns its bibliography and cross-reference choices,
while lanepaper concentrates on the page-level treatment.

\subsection{Lists that carry an argument}
\label{subsec:lists}

An author does not need a special list environment to make a sequence readable.
The standard environments are enough when the sequence has a clear role.
For this paper, the work proceeds in three simple moves:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item establish the hierarchy before adding secondary detail;
  \item give each visual example a reason to be present;
  \item return to the prose so that the example changes understanding.
\end{enumerate}

The same principle applies to a short inline sequence of editorial concerns:
\begin{inlineitem}
  \item measure
  \item contrast
  \item continuity
\end{inlineitem}.
The list is useful because it compresses a sentence, not because it creates a
new kind of content.

\subsection{Citations in the sentence}
\label{subsec:citations}

The bibliography is loaded by the document with \texttt{biblatex}, so a
citation can follow the grammar of the sentence.
\textcite{mittelbach2004latex} presents LaTeX as a system in which structure
and appearance can be separated without losing their connection.
The history of micro-typography makes a related case for small adjustments that
remain subordinate to reading \autocite{thanh2000microtypography}.
These references support the discussion; they are not a second demonstration
layer.

Cross-references work the same way.
The page drawing is discussed in \cref{fig:page-rhythm}, the comparison table
in \cref{tab:signals}, and the illustrative relationship in
\cref{eq:attention}.
\Cref{sec:hierarchy} and \Cref{sec:rhythm} are ordinary sections, so the
document can point readers to them without package-owned reference wrappers.

\subsection{The package boundary}
\label{subsec:boundary}

The surviving lanepaper surface is intentionally narrow.
The package supplies the typographic treatment of ordinary headings, lists,
quotations, floats, and footnotes, along with a small set of title-page and
paragraph tools.
The document supplies the subject, the bibliography, the cross-reference
system, and the decision to use a figure or table.

\begin{description}
  \item[Measure] keeps sustained reading comfortable.
  \item[Contrast] lets headings and notes differ without shouting.
  \item[Continuity] lets standard LaTeX structures remain familiar.
\end{description}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\sectionopening{The best typographic systems become ordinary as soon as they
work.}
They make a long page easier to enter, a dense paragraph easier to follow, and
a reference easier to trust.
They do not require every document to become a tour of their implementation.

This showcase has kept that test in view.
It uses standard sectioning, lists, quotation, figure, table, mathematics, and
cross-reference structures, with the package supplying a consistent treatment
around them.
The result is a paper about reading that also lets the reader see the page being
made.

\textcite{bringhurst2012elements} argues for proportion as a condition of
clarity, while \textcite{brown2018flexible} makes a related case for systems
that do not call attention to their own machinery.
Lanepaper v3 follows the same discipline in code: a narrow public entry point,
ordinary document structures, and enough typographic judgement to make the
whole page cohere.
